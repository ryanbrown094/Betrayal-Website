How to host a traitors-style game at home

A traitors-style game needs no set and no camera crew. A few players are secretly traitors, everyone else tries to find them, the group votes someone out each day, and the traitors pick someone off each night. This guide is how to run one for 8 to 24 friends in your own home: by hand, or the easy way on everyone’s phones.

What is a traitors-style game?

It’s a hidden-role game, also called a social deduction game. The idea is much older than television: parlour games like Mafia (first played in Moscow in the 1980s) and Werewolf work the same way. A small group of players are secretly on the other side. They know each other; nobody else knows them. The majority win by voting every traitor out. The traitors win if even one of them survives to the end.

What makes it work at a party is that nobody can prove anything. There is only what people say, how they vote and how they look when they say it. It suits adults who like a debate, a bluff and a quiet grudge, and it works best with 8 or more people.

What you need

  • 8 to 24 people. Fewer than 8 and the game is over in a couple of rounds; more than 24 and the votes take too long.
  • A way to deal roles in secret. Playing cards work (see below), or a phone each if you use an app.
  • A way to vote in secret. Slips of paper and pens, small whiteboards, or phones.
  • A way to hide the night. Everyone closes their eyes; sleep masks stop peeking.
  • A few party games for the day: a quiz, charades, a guessing game.
  • A prize. Chocolate coins are traditional: they can be counted, shared out and fought over.
  • Time. By hand, allow about 3 to 3½ hours for 8 to 12 players. On phones, where the games are timed and scored for you, it’s nearer an hour.

How many traitors?

About one player in four or five should be a traitor. Too few and they’re caught by the second vote; too many and the loyal players never stand a chance. These are the numbers The Betrayal deals:

Don’t tell the players how many traitors there are. Not knowing is half the fun: every exile raises the question of whether that was the last one.

The roles

The traitors know who the other traitors are. By day they act loyal, steer the vote towards innocent players and quietly lose the games. By night they choose who goes.

The loyal players are everyone else. They know only their own role. They win by finding and voting out every traitor.

The host runs the evening. If you run the game by hand, whoever deals the roles and runs the night knows who the traitors are, so they can’t play. That’s the biggest drawback of doing it by hand: someone has to sit it out.

Players who are out stay at the party. Let them watch, heckle and guess; they often enjoy the rest of the night more than anyone.

How the game runs

  1. Deal the roles in secret. Take one playing card per player: a black card for each traitor and a red card for everyone else. Shuffle, deal face down, and have everyone look at theirs without showing anyone.
  2. Let the traitors find each other. Everyone closes their eyes. The host says “Traitors, open your eyes.” They look around, see each other, and close their eyes again. Then everyone opens their eyes.
  3. Play a game for the prize pot. A quiz round, charades, a guessing game. Every win adds to a shared pot. The traitors will try to lose without being seen to lose.
  4. Hold the vote. Give the table a few minutes to argue on a timer, then everyone writes one name in secret. Reveal the votes together. The player with the most votes is out and reveals whether they were a traitor.
  5. Night falls. Everyone closes their eyes. The host wakes the traitors, who silently point at a victim, then go back to sleep.
  6. Dawn. Everyone opens their eyes and the host names the victim. Give them thirty seconds of last words before they leave the table.
  7. Repeat a game, a vote and a night each round, until about five players are left.
  8. The final and the reveal. The last players decide whether they trust everyone left. If they all do, turn over every card: if only loyal players remain, they win the pot; if a traitor is still at the table, the traitors win. If anyone doubts, hold another vote, this time without showing the role, and ask again.

Getting the vote right

  • Keep it secret until the reveal. If people vote out loud one by one, the last few simply follow the crowd. Written votes, turned over together or one at a time, make every vote count.
  • Put the debate on a clock. Five minutes is plenty. The best accusations come in the last thirty seconds.
  • Decide ties in advance. A quick revote between the tied players, then a coin toss if it ties again.
  • Show the role of whoever goes. It’s the only hard evidence anyone gets all night, and it makes the next vote sharper.

Making the night work

The night is where most home games go wrong: a creaking chair or a rustle of movement gives the traitors away. Keep the room quiet, ask everyone to put their heads down, and make the traitors point, not whisper. While 12 or more players are left, let the traitors choose two victims a night so the game doesn’t drag.

On phones it’s easier: if everyone taps a name on their screen at night, nobody can tell who is really choosing. That’s how The Betrayal does it.

Prizes

A prize makes people lie properly. Put a pot of chocolate coins in the middle and add to it every time the group wins a game. If the loyal players win, they share the pot. For a nastier twist, set a target: if the traitors win, they take what the pot fell short of it, so every game they quietly lost was money in their pocket.

How long it takes

By hand, a round (a game, the vote and the night) takes about 30 to 40 minutes. For 8 to 12 players, plan on about 3 to 3½ hours in all. Bigger groups need more rounds: about 4¼ hours for 16, and 5 to 6 hours for 20 and up. A sample evening for ten:

7.30pmArrivals and drinks
7.45pmThe rules, then the roles are dealt
8pmRound one: a game, the vote, the night
8.40pm to 10pmTwo or three more rounds, with a break for food if you’re eating
10pmThe final with five left
10.45pmThe reveal, the prize and the post-mortem

Tips for the host

  • Explain the rules once, briefly, before the roles are dealt. Everything else can be learnt as you go.
  • Plan the games before anyone arrives. A pile of quiz questions and charades cards saves a lot of dead air.
  • Keep it moving. Timers for the debate, the vote and the last words stop one loud guest running the evening.
  • Sit in a circle, so everyone can see everyone’s face at the vote.
  • Keep the out players involved. Let them guess who the traitors are and see who was right at the end.
  • Make the ending an event. Turn the roles over one at a time. It’s the moment people talk about afterwards.

Questions

How many traitors should there be?

About one player in four or five: 2 traitors for 8 to 10 players, 3 for 11 to 15, 4 for 16 to 22, and 5 for 23 or 24.

Can the host play too?

Not if you run the game by hand, because whoever runs the night knows who the traitors are. If the game runs on phones, as The Betrayal does, the host plays like everyone else and never sees a role.

What is the minimum number of players?

Eight is the practical minimum for a full evening, and The Betrayal needs at least 8. With fewer, the game ends after a couple of rounds.

Do we need a TV?

No. By hand you need only cards, paper and pens. With The Betrayal, a TV shows the questions, the votes and the reveals, with music, but everything can be played on the phones alone.
